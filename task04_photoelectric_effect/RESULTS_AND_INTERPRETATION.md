## Main conclusion

The work function sets whether photoemission is possible, while photon energy
above that threshold sets the maximum electron kinetic energy and therefore
the stopping potential.

For every ideal metal in the official table:

$$
eV_s=hf-W.
$$

Consequently:

1. increasing frequency raises stopping potential linearly;
2. every metal has the same frequency gradient $h/e$;
3. increasing the work function shifts the parallel line to a higher threshold
   frequency and a shorter threshold wavelength; and
4. below threshold, there are no emitted photoelectrons, so a physical stopping
   potential is undefined rather than negative.

These conclusions describe the stated ideal photoelectric model. They are not
claims that every real surface has exactly the one-decimal work function printed
in the challenge presentation.

## Governing relations and units

The maximum kinetic energy of an emitted electron is

$$
K_{\max}=hf-W.
$$

A reverse stopping potential removes that maximum kinetic energy:

$$
eV_s=K_{\max}.
$$

Therefore, in the frequency coordinate,

$$
V_s=\frac{h}{e}f-\frac{W}{e}.
$$

If the work function is entered numerically in electronvolts, dividing its
energy by the elementary charge gives the same numerical value in volts. The
implemented equation can therefore be read as

$$
V_s\,[\mathrm V]
=\left(4.135667696923859\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{V\,s}\right)f
-W\,[\mathrm{eV}].
$$

The common gradient is

$$
\frac{h}{e}=4.135667696923859\ \mathrm{V}
\quad\text{per}\quad 10^{15}\ \mathrm{Hz}.
$$

Using the vacuum relation $f=c/\lambda$ gives

$$
V_s=\frac{hc}{e\lambda}-\frac{W}{e}.
$$

The exact analytical thresholds are

$$
f_0=\frac{W}{h},
\qquad
\lambda_0=\frac{hc}{W}.
$$

Photoemission requires $f\geq f_0$, equivalently
$\lambda\leq\lambda_0$.

## Interpretation of the frequency graph

The required frequency graph consists of parallel straight lines. Their shared
gradient follows directly from differentiating the model:

$$
\frac{\mathrm dV_s}{\mathrm df}=\frac{h}{e}.
$$

Changing the metal changes the work-function term, not the gradient. At a fixed
frequency, a smaller work function leaves more photon energy as electron kinetic
energy, producing a larger stopping potential.

At the frozen anchor $f=1.5\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{Hz}$, every official metal
emits in the ideal model:

| Material or group | $V_s$ / V |
| --- | ---: |
| Ag, Al, Pb | $1.903502$ |
| Au | $1.103502$ |
| Cu | $1.503502$ |
| Sn | $1.803502$ |
| W | $1.703502$ |
| Ni | $1.603502$ |
| Na | $3.803502$ |

For example, sodium and gold differ in work function by
$5.1-2.4=2.7\ \mathrm{eV}$. Their stopping potentials at the same frequency
also differ by $2.7\ \mathrm V$. That equality is a consequence of measuring
energy in electronvolts and then dividing by the charge of one electron.

The physical lines begin at their analytical threshold dots. The graph does not
draw a horizontal zero-voltage segment to the left of a threshold because that
would imply emitted electrons with zero maximum energy. In fact, there are no
photoelectrons there, so there is no stopping potential to measure.

## Interpretation of the wavelength graph

The wavelength graph is curved rather than linear because photon energy varies
as $1/\lambda$:

$$
V_s=\frac{hc}{e\lambda}-\frac{W}{e}.
$$

Increasing wavelength lowers photon energy. Each physical curve therefore
decreases until it reaches zero at $\lambda_0$, then disappears. A point to the
right of the cut-off represents no photoemission, not a zero-valued physical
curve.

Using the declared visible range $380$--$750\ \mathrm{nm}$, sodium is the only
official entry whose cut-off lies in visible light:

$$
\lambda_{0,\mathrm{Na}}=516.601\ \mathrm{nm}.
$$

This does not mean that every visible wavelength ejects electrons from sodium.
Within the ideal model, visible photoemission occurs only for the higher-energy
part of the visible range, $380\ \mathrm{nm}\leq\lambda\leq516.601\ \mathrm{nm}$.
All eight other source entries have $\lambda_0<380\ \mathrm{nm}$ and therefore
require ultraviolet photons in this ideal comparison.

At the supporting anchor $\lambda=200\ \mathrm{nm}$, all nine metals emit. The
calculated stopping potentials are $1.899210\ \mathrm V$ for Ag/Al/Pb,
$1.099210\ \mathrm V$ for Au, $1.499210\ \mathrm V$ for Cu,
$1.799210\ \mathrm V$ for Sn, $1.699210\ \mathrm V$ for W,
$1.599210\ \mathrm V$ for Ni, and $3.799210\ \mathrm V$ for Na.

## Copper threshold and the meaning of a negative line

Copper is the official worked example with $W=4.7\ \mathrm{eV}$. The model gives

$$
f_0=1.136454943780\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{Hz},
\qquad
\lambda_0=263.796166879\ \mathrm{nm}.
$$

At this threshold, $hf=W$, so the maximum kinetic energy and stopping potential
are both zero. Above threshold, the solid line is physical. Below threshold,
the algebraic expression $hf/e-W/e$ is negative, but this negative value is an
energy deficit: one photon does not contain enough energy to liberate an
electron.

The dashed negative section in the copper figure is therefore labelled
“mathematical extrapolation: no photoemission.” It is useful for showing the
straight-line intercept, but it is not a negative stopping-potential
measurement.

## What stopping potential does not describe

Stopping potential measures the maximum kinetic energy per unit charge of the
emitted electrons. It does not by itself give the number of emitted electrons
or the photocurrent.

In the simple one-photon model, increasing light intensity at fixed frequency
above threshold can increase the number of photoelectrons, but it does not
increase the maximum energy $hf-W$ of each emitted electron. Therefore this
Task 4 calculation correctly varies frequency or wavelength and metal work
function; it does not infer current or intensity dependence from the stopping
potential curves.

## Assumptions and limitations

### Physical assumptions

- One photon transfers its energy to one electron.
- The incident-light wavelength is the vacuum wavelength.
- Each ideal metal has one uniform work function equal to the supplied
  one-decimal value.
- The maximum-energy electron reaches the surface without additional energy
  loss.
- The reverse potential exactly balances that maximum kinetic energy.
- Contact potentials, apparatus geometry, electric-field nonuniformity, and
  space charge are absent.
- The work function is treated as independent of temperature and surface
  history.
- Multiphoton and relativistic effects are negligible.

### Important real-surface limitations

Real work functions depend on crystal face, surface cleanliness, adsorbates,
oxidation, defects, and experimental conditions. The emitted electrons also
have an energy distribution because they originate in different states and may
lose energy before escaping. The model predicts the maximum kinetic energy, not
that every emitted electron has the same energy.

Real apparatus can introduce contact-potential corrections, nonuniform fields,
dark current, finite energy resolution, and uncertainty in determining the
zero-current stopping point. None of these effects is represented here.

### Numerical limitations

- Calculations use double-precision floating-point arithmetic.
- Displayed curves connect points on finite declared grids; the connecting
  segments are visualization, not additional measurements.
- Cut-offs are analytical values, not estimates from the nearest grid point.
- The many digits stored in derived values provide computational
  reproducibility; they do not imply that a one-decimal source work function is
  experimentally known to that precision.
- No experimental uncertainty or statistical fit is present because no
  measured dataset was supplied or used.

## What the validation proves and does not prove

The 43 checks establish that the implementation is internally consistent with
the declared constants, equations, units, source values, physical domains,
threshold inequalities, analytical cut-offs, and coordinate transformation.
They make unit errors, sign errors, incorrect masking, and inconsistent
frequency/wavelength calculations much less likely.

They do not experimentally validate the ideal work-function table or prove
that omitted surface and apparatus effects are negligible in a laboratory.
Numerical model validation and experimental validation are different tasks.
Our defensible conclusion is that the code correctly implements the stated
ideal photoelectric stopping-potential model over the declared domains.

## Final scientific conclusion

The completed simulation shows that Einstein's photoelectric equation produces
parallel stopping-potential lines against frequency, with the common gradient
$h/e$ and metal-dependent threshold $W/h$. Expressing the same calculation
against wavelength produces decreasing $1/\lambda$ curves that end at
$hc/W$. The official source values place only sodium's ideal cut-off in the
visible range, while the other metals require ultraviolet light.

The agreement between the two coordinates, analytical thresholds, scalar
anchors, physical masks, deterministic evidence, and 43-check validation report
supports the claim that the required Task 4 model has been implemented and
interpreted correctly within its stated assumptions.

For the final presentation, the defensible one-sentence takeaway is:

> Photon energy above the metal's work function becomes maximum electron
> kinetic energy, so every metal shares the gradient $h/e$ but has its own
> photoemission threshold.
