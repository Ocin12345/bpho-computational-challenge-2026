About

Origin

It started with the code.

This website presents my submission for the 2026 British Physics Olympiad Computational Challenge, centred on the theme of quantum physics.

The project brings together ten computational tasks exploring how mathematical models, numerical methods, and interactive simulations can be used to investigate physical phenomena that are often difficult to observe directly.

The project began with scientific models written in Python. Although these programs could calculate results and produce accurate graphs, they did not fully communicate how the underlying physical systems behaved. Many quantum phenomena become easier to understand when their evolution can be observed and their parameters adjusted in real time.

I therefore developed this website to make the project more accessible and exploratory. A browser-based platform allows the simulations, explanations, results, and supporting materials to be brought together in one place without requiring visitors to install specialist software.

The website does not replace the mathematical work behind the project. Instead, it provides a visual interface through which that work can be investigated, questioned, and understood. Visitors can explore the individual tasks, examine extensions of the original problems, read the accompanying research paper, and attempt related problem sets.

Method

The physics comes first.

Each task begins with the relevant physical principles and mathematical relationships. These are translated into computational models, tested against analytical results where possible, and examined across a range of parameters to ensure that their behaviour remains physically reasonable.

The project distinguishes between established physics, numerical approximations, and exploratory extensions. Assumptions and limitations are recorded alongside the results, while automated tests, convergence checks, conservation laws, and comparisons with independent calculations are used where appropriate.

The aim is not simply to produce convincing animations, but to create models whose behaviour can be explained, tested, and justified.

Ten

Ten problems. One thread.

The project is organised around ten computational challenges, progressing from statistical motion and thermal radiation to quantum systems and modern applications. Each task brings together the underlying theory, computational model, numerical results, visual evidence, and an explanation of the model's assumptions and limitations.

  1. 01Random walks
  2. 02Brownian motion
  3. 03Thermal radiation
  4. 04Photoelectric effect
  5. 05Atomic spectra
  6. 06Electron diffraction
  7. 07Particles in a box
  8. 08Quantum cryptography
  9. 09Compton scattering
  10. 10Hydrogenic orbitals

Beyond the required tasks, the website also contains extended investigations, an accompanying research paper, and original problem sets. These sections develop selected ideas in greater depth and connect the computational results to broader questions in physics.

Seeing

Make the invisible move.

Quantum physics is frequently presented through dense notation and static diagrams.

While these are essential, they can make it difficult to develop an intuitive understanding of how a system changes or responds to different conditions. The website therefore treats interaction as a form of scientific explanation. Animation, responsive controls, and data visualisation allow visitors to observe relationships that may be less obvious from equations alone.

The visual design draws inspiration from wavefunctions, interference patterns, probability fields, and atomic structure, giving the project a consistent identity rooted in its scientific subject. Clarity remains more important than spectacle. Every visual element should help communicate the physics, direct attention to meaningful results, or make the project easier to navigate.

The aim is to create an experience that feels imaginative and engaging while remaining scientifically disciplined.

Still curious

Build it. Break it. Check it.

Although this project was created for the BPhO Computational Challenge, its broader purpose extends beyond the competition. It demonstrates how computation can connect mathematical theory, numerical experimentation, and visual explanation within a single scientific experience.

Future development could include more advanced simulations, deeper extensions of selected tasks, additional problem sets, and improved tools for comparing theoretical predictions with numerical results. The website can therefore continue to grow as both a record of the original submission and a platform for further exploration.

Ultimately, I hope the project encourages visitors to see quantum physics not as a collection of inaccessible equations, but as a subject that can be investigated, visualised, and understood through careful computation.

I would like to thank the British Physics Olympiad for creating the Computational Challenge and for providing an opportunity to explore physics through programming, numerical modelling, and scientific communication.

I am also grateful to the teachers and members of the academic community at Shenzhen College of International Education who have supported my study of physics and encouraged my interest in computational research.

This project makes use of open-source scientific and web-development tools. All external resources and references used in the scientific work will be credited in the relevant task documentation and research paper.
